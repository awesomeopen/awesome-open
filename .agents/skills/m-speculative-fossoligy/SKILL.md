# Speculative FOSSoligy macro

Draw unfamiliar human worlds as if they have been carefully observed, inhabited, and used. Make the situation strange; make its human purpose lucid. Preserve the user's spelling, **Speculative FOSSoligy macro**, and the invocation name `m-speculative-fossoligy`.

This variant of Speculative FOSSoligy expands the view from an object to the people, places, practices, and consequences around it. Let tools and ideas emerge from human making, use, memory, imagination, exchange, or consumption. “Macro” describes this wider field of relationships; choose the scale the subject needs, from a hand at work to an inhabited landscape.

Use an independent drawing language. Read `references/style-atlas.md` when developing a new family of subjects, resolving visual drift, or explaining the sources. Retain its observational linework, connected construction, and selective color. Apply the human-centric scope throughout composition and subject selection.

## Establish the task

1. Choose **redraw** when the user supplies a target image, or **invent** when they supply a description. Distinguish a style reference from the actual target. Inspect all target images before drafting the generation prompt.
2. Retain stated subject, framing, colors, output dimensions, transparency, exact wording, and edit constraints. User constraints outrank defaults. Ask only when a missing image or genuinely ambiguous target prevents execution.
3. Default to one finished drawing with one principal human situation and a clear focal action. Welcome portraits, groups, interiors, dwellings, streets, cities, landscapes, and historical or imagined scenes. Choose framing and aspect ratio to show the relevant relationships. Use a clean white or cool pale ground where the scene opens into whitespace.
4. Establish who makes, uses, remembers, imagines, maintains, shares, or consumes the subject, and what changes for them. Include people engaged in that activity when it helps explain the idea. An unoccupied scene can carry human presence through specific traces of use, repair, ritual, habitation, or history. Make these traces central and legible. Preserve the user's requested subject and composition.
5. Use the available image-generation/editing tool for the final raster art. Follow its reference-image and transparency requirements; never substitute a prose prompt for an image unless the user asks for a prompt. When image generation is unavailable, say so and supply a usable prompt without claiming completion. Preserve transparency on edits unless requested otherwise.

## The visual grammar

### 1. Recognition before surprise

Give the scene a readable hierarchy: people and action, the tool or shared structure, then the environment. Use strong contours and clear masses. At thumbnail size, the human activity and its relationship to the project should read. Add protrusions only when the subject calls for them; compact, enclosed, suspended, stacked, and open structures are equally valid. At thumbnail size, the subject category and posture/function should remain legible. Use asymmetry when it suggests growth, use, adaptation, or repair; avoid ornamental imbalance everywhere.

### 2. One governing impossibility

For invention, choose one consequential oddity: a communal roof folds to gather conversations; an archive grows walkable layers as people add memories; a neighborhood shares stored rain through jointed courtyards. Extend that rule into only the supporting details it needs; do not turn a suggested detail count into a fixed number of limbs, branches, tools, or components. Connect structures through shared material, gradual anatomical transitions, believable joints, or matching attachment surfaces. Do not paste an unrelated animal head onto an appliance and call the combination complete.

Let the drawing answer at least one question without text: Who uses this, and how does it help them work, live, remember, think, travel, exchange, or consume? Suggest local cause and effect without pretending the object is scientifically possible. A purely biological organism need not contain manufactured parts; a tool need not be alive.

For a faithful redraw, do not impose this impossibility by changing protected geometry. Carry strangeness through color, line, material interpretation, and the observational presentation. Structural mutation requires authorization.

### 3. Observational drawing with graphic discipline

Use fine, slightly variable dark ink or pencil-like contours; selective crosshatching, stippling, and colored-pencil grain; translucent color with occasional flat, saturated regions. Make the handmade quality reside in marks and small decisions, not an applied distress filter. Describe volume with economical hatching and modest internal shading. Keep edges crisp enough to read.

Vary contour weight: firmer at overlaps and contact, delicate on membranes and fine appendages. Leave quiet areas unworked. Avoid glossy CGI, cinematic lighting, photographic surfaces, airbrushed fantasy polish, uniformly heavy cartoon outlines, and fake antique paper stains.

### 4. Color that describes anatomy or operation

Start with white or cool pale ground, charcoal/umber linework, and three to five main colors. Carry the palette through skin, clothing, architecture, and terrain with a clear value hierarchy. Suggested families: moss + oxidized turquoise + ochre + coral; ink blue + leaf green + vermilion + cream; plum + teal + straw yellow. These are options, not fixed brand colors.

Place one high-chroma passage where the subject acts, opens, senses, or changes material. Allow unlikely but deliberate color relationships. Use repeated bands, dots, scales, or ribs to distinguish tissues or components. Reserve pattern for meaningful surfaces, rather than covering the whole drawing. Retain specified source colors during a constrained redraw. A psychedelic variant may expand saturation, but must preserve contour and value hierarchy.

### 5. Controlled abundance

Concentrate intricate marks in a small number of useful areas, such as a hinge, sensory organ, folded edge, or junction. Keep the principal mass calmer. Prefer one unexpected secondary discovery over many unrelated curiosities. Let a close look reveal a detail that explains the main form.

### 6. Calm attention toward an odd subject

Aim for curiosity, dry amusement, and a little unease. Show people absorbed in meaningful activities, with observed posture, gestures, expressions, clothing, and social relationships. Give figures agency and a credible place in the scene. Present the situation matter-of-factly; do not dramatize its importance. Avoid heroic staging, magical glow, cute mascot defaults, stock promotional smiles, random tentacles, ubiquitous eyeballs, decorative gears, and horror by default. A toy can be playful without becoming a character; an animal can be expressive without human gestures.

## Redraw workflow

Before generation, make a short internal preservation list: subject identity, count, pose, silhouette, orientation, proportions, attachment points, defining parts, composition, text, and colors that the user requires.

- **Faithful (default):** Preserve identity, anatomy/geometry, pose, count, and composition. Change drawing medium, rendering, and unconstrained color treatment. Do not grow extra limbs, add mechanisms, or replace parts merely to increase strangeness.
- **Interpretive (only when invited):** Preserve the identifying silhouette and defining parts; reinterpret selected structures through one coherent transformation. Keep protected details exact.
- **Free rein (only when invited):** Rebuild morphology around the described function and one governing impossibility, while retaining explicitly requested anchors.

Use the original target as the image-edit reference. Describe preservation constraints before style instructions. Preserve people, inhabited settings, landscapes, and spatial relationships in scenic inputs. Isolate an object only when requested. For a faithful object-only redraw, express human use through existing affordances and wear; add people or surroundings only when the requested edit permits composition changes.

For follow-up edits, preserve the accepted version and change only the requested aspect. Avoid restarting the design or silently increasing mutation on each pass.

## Invent workflow

1. Read the supplied brief or project sources. Identify its actual purpose, intended users, and one concrete situation. Keep sourced facts distinct from the invented visual metaphor; historical details should follow evidence, and imagined settings should read as interpretations.
2. Choose a human lens: making/use, history/memory, ideation/learning, or exchange/consumption. Write one internal sentence: “These people [do something meaningful] through [one unfamiliar mechanism or spatial relationship].”
3. Choose the scale that explains it: hands using a tool, a room of collaborators, an inhabited dwelling, a street, a city system, or a worked landscape. A human figure should contribute an action or relationship; choose purposeful traces of human activity when figures are absent.
4. Establish connected construction, plausible support, circulation, reach, and scale. Keep human anatomy and gestures legible. Place the governing impossibility in the system, environment, or artifact; transform bodies only when the concept or user calls for it.
5. Build composition around the focal action and its consequence. Use paths, sightlines, thresholds, material flows, or layers of history to connect people and place. Keep secondary activity subordinate.
6. Choose a view that exposes that relationship: an intimate view of hands, an eye-level encounter, an inhabited section, a street perspective, or a territorial overview. Add an inset or short sequence only when it clarifies use or change over time.
7. Apply the visual grammar and generate the image.

## Variation across drawings and batches

Keep the style recognizable through drawing language and internal logic, not a recurring body plan. Apply this to consecutive requests as well as explicit batches such as “10 logos for these 10 software tools.” Do not carry the previous prompt's composition into the next concept by inertia.

For a batch, plan all items briefly before generating the first image. Keep an internal table with one row per item: actual function, human purpose, participants or traces of use, setting and scale, metaphor/governing rule, composition, view, organic/manufactured balance, and focal color placement. Derive each concept independently from its subject. For tools and projects, identify each one's distinguishing human situation. Vary intimate use, collective work, historical layers, domestic life, and civic or landscape systems as the subjects warrant. For requested logos, compress that relationship into a readable mark.

- Deliberately vary silhouette, orientation, component count, and spatial arrangement across the set. Use meaningful differences such as compact versus elongated, enclosed versus open, stacked versus nested, front-facing versus profile, or suspended versus grounded. These are options, not a mandatory rotation or template list.
- Compare each proposal with both neighboring items and the set as a whole. Revise accidental repeats of the same main mass, appendage layout, or operational metaphor. Mirroring, recoloring, or changing surface ornament alone does not establish a distinct design.
- Specifically check for the observed failure of “three branches growing horizontally out of the right side.” It is not a signature of this style. Also watch for repeated shells, spirals, plant-machine hybrids, and left-input/right-output apparatuses. Use any of these when justified, without making them defaults.
- Preserve family resemblance through compatible linework, texture, detail hierarchy, framing, and, when appropriate, a shared palette. Do not force every subject to share anatomy, machinery, materials, or accent placement.
- Let function determine part counts and arrangement. Do not invent unnecessary parts, distort the concept, or randomize geometry merely to satisfy variety. A machine can remain entirely manufactured; an organism can remain entirely biological.
- For faithful redraws, preserve the supplied silhouettes, counts, orientations, and composition. Vary only unconstrained treatment. For requested variants of one established mark, preserve its identifying structure and vary only the permitted axes; continuity takes precedence over diversity.

Generate one separate image per requested item unless the user asks for a combined sheet. In each prompt, specify that item's own structure and composition explicitly. After generation, compare the actual silhouettes and arrangements across the batch; correct clear accidental duplication with targeted edits. Do not regenerate accepted designs solely to maximize novelty.

## Adapt to subject category

| Subject | Develop |
|---|---|
| People | Purposeful action, observed anatomy, gesture, clothing, relationships, and individual agency |
| Tool or machine | Human handling, maintenance, working ends, visible force or material flow, and the result of use |
| Home or dwelling | Occupants, thresholds, domestic routines, repair, shelter, and shared resources |
| City or settlement | Streets, services, gathering, movement, exchange, and a coherent spatial system |
| Landscape | Human routes, cultivation, stewardship, extraction, recreation, or accumulated history, with legible terrain |
| History or institution | Specific practices, artifacts, records, succession, and material evidence of time |
| Idea or learning | People testing, debating, building, rehearsing, or revising something through a visible action |
| Consumption or exchange | Preparation, distribution, selection, sharing, use, and what remains afterward |
| Animal or plant | A coherent organism within human care, cultivation, observation, coexistence, or livelihood |

## Prompt construction

Build a concrete prompt in this order:

1. Target and mode; exact preservation constraints for edits.
2. Human purpose, participants or traces of activity, setting, view, and governing oddity if invention is allowed.
3. Connected construction and the specific details that explain it.
4. Drawing medium, line hierarchy, selective texture, palette, and focal accent.
5. Framing, background/transparency, whitespace, and any authorized inset.
6. Short exclusions relevant to this specific request.

Describe the traits explicitly; the invented style name alone carries no reliable instructions. Do not rely on artist-name lists or “surreal/psychedelic” alone. Do not copy recognizable reference creatures, compositions, or source glyphs. Omit lettering by default. If requested, use the user's actual readable labels; reserve invented script for an explicit request and never let it substitute for structure.

## Check the result

Inspect the generated image when the tool permits, and correct clear mismatches with a focused edit:

- Does the human purpose read at a glance? Are people acting, or do specific traces explain human use, history, ideation, or consumption?
- Do people, objects, architecture, and terrain share a coherent scale and spatial relationship? Does the setting help explain the target?
- For redraws, did the identity, pose, count, and protected details survive?
- Is the governing oddity coherent, with connected joints and believable support?
- Are marks drawn rather than glossy, muddy, uniformly noisy, or mechanically perfect?
- Does concentrated detail reward inspection while leaving calm areas?
- Does color separate functional regions without overwhelming the contour?
- Is it an original subject rather than a close replica of a reference motif?
- Across recent drawings or a batch, does it have a purpose-specific silhouette and arrangement, rather than a recolored or mirrored repeat? Preserve intentional continuity and redraw constraints.
- Is the output actually an image, at the requested framing and transparency?

Fix structure before embellishment. Do not keep regenerating merely for novelty. Deliver the drawing with minimal prose. Explain concept choices only if useful or requested.
